A dashboard that nobody opens is a failed investment. The challenge at Robi Axiata wasn't building charts — it was building dashboards that a 17-person field team wanted to check every morning, and that management trusted enough to act on.

## Designing for the daily habit

The field team didn't have time for deep exploration. They needed **today's numbers versus yesterday's**, in context, with the "why" attached. So the dashboards were built around three rhythms:

- **Daily** — snapshots of yesterday's performance per team member, with anomalies flagged.
- **Weekly** — trends, route-level breakdowns, and coverage gaps.
- **Monthly** — the full picture for regional leadership and planning.

## What made them work

1. **Single source of truth** — outputs cross-validated against Salesforce CRM records and field-reported sales, so the numbers could be trusted.
2. **Action framing** — every view answered a question ("where do I focus today?") rather than just displaying data.
3. **Design discipline** — consistent visual language: one color for targets, clear hierarchy, the metric up top.

## The outcome

The dashboards became the data backbone for territory decisions, feeding directly into the 18% sales increase and the national ranking of 13th out of 200+ officers. The proof that it worked: **people kept opening them.**

## Takeaway

Good dashboard design is a business-analysis skill before it is a visualization skill. Knowing what decision each screen is meant to support — and who will make it — is what separates dashboards that inform from dashboards that collect dust.

> Trust in reports comes from verification, not just visualization.
