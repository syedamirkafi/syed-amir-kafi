A report that nobody opens is a failed investment. The challenge at Robi Axiata wasn't building charts — it was building a report suite a 17-person territory team wanted to check every morning, and that management trusted enough to act on.

## The report suite

The team didn't have time for deep exploration. They needed **today's numbers versus target**, in context, with the "why" attached. So the reports were built around three rhythms:

- **Daily — rep scorecards.** Each sales rep got their own card: yesterday's sales against target, with KPI numbers up top and a small bar showing how far along they were. Open it, know where you stand, pick where to go today.
- **Weekly — route rankings.** Routes ranked by revenue, growth, and coverage cost, with trend lines versus a target line. Management read this to see which corridors deserved more reps.
- **Monthly — shop-level detail.** The full picture per retailer: who sells, who doesn't, and where stock is sitting. This fed the next month's promotion and coverage plan.

Every screen answered a question — "where do I focus today?" — rather than just displaying data.

## What made them work

1. **Single source of truth** — outputs cross-validated against Salesforce CRM records and field-reported sales, so the numbers could be trusted.
2. **Action framing** — every view answered a decision, not just a curiosity.
3. **Design discipline** — consistent visual language: one color for targets, clear hierarchy, the metric up top.

## The outcome

The reports became the data backbone for territory decisions, feeding directly into the 18% sales increase and the national ranking of 13th out of 200+ officers. The proof that it worked: **people kept opening them.**

## Takeaway

Good report design is a business-analysis skill before it is a visualization skill. Knowing what decision each screen is meant to support — and who will make it — is what separates reports that inform from reports that collect dust.

> Trust in reports comes from verification, not just visualization.
