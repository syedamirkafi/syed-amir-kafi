A friend's mechanical-keyboard business was growing out of a hobby: stock lived in messages, prices in memory, and "are we making money?" was a guess. As Operations Team Lead at KeyB Imports, my first job was to make the money honest.

## The problem

Every sale, restock, and pricing decision was ad hoc. We couldn't answer the three questions any small operation needs to answer:

- **What do we have?** Stock levels lived in someone's head.
- **What does it cost?** Prices were set per-sale, not per-SKU.
- **Are we making money?** Cash in versus out had never been reconciled.

## The system

One spreadsheet, structured as a table every SKU could live in:

- **Stock level** per SKU, with a low-stock flag so restock happened before a sale was lost.
- **A price per SKU**, set with the cost in view — not off the top of the head.
- **Money in versus out** tracked alongside, so the business knew whether a group buy or a new product line actually earned.

## The result

Restock and pricing decisions moved from guesswork to a single table. When the QK65 group buy and a new dust-cover line came in, the stock and the money around them were already trackable — the operation could grow without losing its numbers.

## The reflection

The tool was a spreadsheet. The real work was **deciding what to track** and making it so simple that the founder would actually keep it updated. Operations systems fail when they're built for the analyst, not the person who has to live in them.

> A system that's good enough to keep updated beats a perfect system nobody maintains.

That lesson — build the simplest system the operation will actually use — is the same one I bring to process maps and requirements today.
